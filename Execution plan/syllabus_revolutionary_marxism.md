# Revolutionary Marxism: A Self-Study Syllabus

*(Marxism as strategy, organisation, and state power — the political-revolutionary tradition from the Second International through Bolshevism, Stalinism, and Maoism to the socialist "woman question.")*

**Goal:** Working command of the strand of Marxism the three companion syllabis deliberately exclude — Marxism not as economics (that is the *Marxist Political Economy* syllabus), not as analytic social philosophy (the *Analytical Marxism* syllabus), and not as critical social theory (Sozialphilosophie Modules 13–14 / Appendix C), but as a **theory-and-practice of revolution**: who is the revolutionary subject, how is a movement organised, is the road reform or rupture, does one smash or capture the state, and — the question no Western-Marxist syllabus touches — how is a post-capitalist society actually constructed and governed. This is the tradition of Bernstein and Luxemburg, Lenin and Trotsky, Stalin and Mao, Zetkin and Kollontai.

**Assumed background:** Prior reading of Marx (at least *Kapital* Bd. 1 chs. 1–3 and the *Manifest der Kommunistischen Partei*). German and English reading fluency (most primary texts here exist in both; Russian, Italian, and Chinese originals are read in translation). No economics prerequisite — unlike the companion MPE syllabus, the register here is political and strategic, not quantitative. Familiarity with the Sozialphilosophie programme's Appendix C helps but is not required.

**Note on genre — read this first.** These texts are not treatises. They are *interventions*: pamphlets, party programmes, factional polemics, "theses," directives written to win an argument inside a movement and change what people do. Reading *What Is to Be Done?* or *State and Revolution* the way you read Hegel or Adorno — as a system to be reconstructed — misreads them. Their criterion of success is not "is it true / coherent?" but "did it build the movement / did it take and hold power?" The tradition is also wildly uneven in intellectual quality: some texts (Lenin on Hegel, Trotsky on uneven development, Zetkin on fascism) reward close philosophical reading; others (Stalin's *Diamat*, Lenin's *Materialism and Empirio-Criticism*) are worth reading **only as symptoms** — documents of the codification of Marxism into a state catechism — and should never receive Full-tier Practice A (four-part argument reconstruction) effort. The tags and notes below mark which is which.

**Tags:** [P] Primary · [ES] Essential secondary · [RS] Recommended secondary · [O] Orientation · [NE] Non-essential · **[HY] High-Yield** (overlay tag — the item pays back directly into your Phase III publication target or an existing programme spine; see the High-Yield Path below) · **[SYM] Symptom-only** (read as historical document, not for doctrinal content).

## Relationship to the companion syllabis and the main programme

This is the **fourth wing**. The division of labour is clean, and the point of the flags is to read each shared text once, in the register that owns it:

- **Marxist Political Economy syllabus** owns imperialism-*as-economics* (Hilferding, Luxemburg's *Akkumulation*, the unequal-exchange "drain"), crisis theory, and value. Where Lenin's or Bukharin's *Imperialism* appears **here**, it is read for the *political-strategic* thesis (the epoch of wars and revolutions, the labour aristocracy, the weakest-link theory). `[→ spine B]`
- **Analytical Marxism syllabus** owns the *feasibility / institutional-design* version of the transition (Wright's real utopias, Roemer, Van Parijs). This syllabus owns the *historical-strategic* version — permanent revolution, War Communism / NEP, "primitive socialist accumulation," New Democracy — i.e. what transition looked like when a movement was actually attempting it.
- **Sozialphilosophie Appendix C / Modules 13–14** owns Western Marxism *as philosophy and critical theory* (Lukács, Korsch, Gramsci-of-the-Notebooks, Althusser, the *neue Marx-Lektüre*, operaismo, eco-Marxism, the Budapest School). This syllabus is the **political-historical substrate underneath** it: the conjuncture and the strategic debates those thinkers were responding to. On Perry Anderson's thesis (your Appendix C.0.1, *Considerations on Western Marxism*), Western Marxism *is the flight from* exactly this tradition — "from politics to philosophy, from economics to culture" — after the defeat of the 1917–23 revolutionary wave. Reading this syllabus is reading the absent cause of your Appendix C.

**Shared nodes read once, in two registers.** Lukács (politically here, epistemologically there), Gramsci (*Ordine Nuovo* here, *Quaderni* there), council communism → operaismo (C.VI), the woman question (Zetkin/Kollontai here → Federici C.16–C.17 there), and imperialism (politics here → economics in MPE Module 4).

**The productive asymmetry.** What this tradition possesses and your other three lack is a worked-out theory of **agency, organisation, the transition, and the state** — the downstream questions the diagnostic traditions bracket. A normative-reconstruction endpoint that eventually asks "so what replaces this, and who brings it about?" (Honneth, *Die Idee des Sozialismus*; the "real utopias" literature) has to walk back into exactly this terrain. That is the deepest reason it belongs on the shelf, even if most of it stays at Orientation depth.

## MODULE 0: ORIENTATION — "MARXISM AFTER MARX" AND HOW ORTHODOXY WAS MANUFACTURED

**Why this matters:** There is no "Marxism" in the singular before it is *made* into one — into a *Weltanschauung* (worldview), a party doctrine, an -ism. That manufacturing is the work of the late Engels and of Kautsky, and everything downstream (Bolshevik "orthodoxy," Bernstein's "revisionism," Stalin's *Diamat*) is a position in the field they opened. Start here so that the later texts read as *moves in a game* rather than as free-standing theories. This module is mostly reference and orientation; do not read it through.

## MODULE 1: THE SECOND INTERNATIONAL — ORTHODOXY, REVISIONISM, AND REFORM VS. REVOLUTION (c. 1889–1914)

**Why this matters:** Every strategic disagreement in twentieth-century Marxism is prefigured here, in the German Social Democratic Party (SPD) — the mass party that *was* Marxism before 1914. The revisionism controversy (Bernstein) and the reform-vs-revolution controversy (Luxemburg) are not antiquarian: they are the same dispute that returns as Eurocommunism, as Wright's transformation strategies (your Analytical-Marxism 4.3), and as Honneth's *Die Idee des Sozialismus*. Read this module as the exposition of a problem the tradition never solves.

| # | Text | Type | Est. h | Notes |
|---|------|------|--------|-------|
| 1.1 | Eduard Bernstein, *Die Voraussetzungen des Sozialismus und die Aufgaben der Sozialdemokratie* (1899; English: *The Preconditions of Socialism* / *Evolutionary Socialism*) | [P] **[HY]** | 6–8 | The founding text of social-democratic reformism and the first serious *internal* revision of Marx: the breakdown ("*Zusammenbruch*") theory is empirically false, the middle classes are not disappearing, crises are moderating — therefore socialism is an ethical goal reached by gradual, legal reform, not a catastrophic transition. "The movement is everything, the final goal nothing." The permanent antipode to everything else in this syllabus. |
| 1.2 | Rosa Luxemburg, *Sozialreform oder Revolution?* (1899/1900) | [P] **[HY]** | 4–6 | The canonical reply to Bernstein: reforms are not "installments" of socialism but the by-product of a struggle whose logic is revolutionary; abandon the goal and reform work itself loses its meaning. The clearest short statement of why the reform/revolution distinction is not a matter of tempo but of two different theories of social change. Read 1.1 and 1.2 as a paired exchange. |
| 1.3 | Luxemburg, *Massenstreik, Partei und Gewerkschaften* (1906) | [P] | 5–7 | The theory of the mass strike as the spontaneous form of revolutionary self-education of the class — the direct counter-model to Lenin's organisational answer in Module 2. The spontaneity-vs-organisation axis is born here. |
| 1.4 | Karl Kautsky, *Der Weg zur Macht* (1909) | [P] | 6–8 | The "orthodox centre" at its most militant: the theory of the party as patiently building strength toward an inevitable conquest of power — neither Bernstein's gradualism nor Luxemburg's spontaneism. Kautsky was *the* authority ("the Pope of Marxism") until 1914; understanding why Lenin later reviled him (see 2.4's companion, *The Proletarian Revolution and the Renegade Kautsky*) requires reading him at his strongest first. |
| 1.5 | Luxemburg, *Die Russische Revolution* (1918) | [RS] | 3–4 | Her prison critique of the Bolsheviks' suppression of democracy — "*Freiheit ist immer die Freiheit des Andersdenkenden*" (freedom is always the freedom of the one who thinks differently). The left-wing critique of Leninism *from within* the revolutionary camp; indispensable counterweight to Module 2 and a bridge to the council-communist pole in Module 3. |

**Module total: 24–33 h.**

**[Economist's note]** Bernstein's argument is, structurally, an empirical-forecasting dispute: he bets that the data (concentration, immiseration, crisis frequency) will not vindicate the *Zusammenbruch* model. The orthodox reply is not primarily "your data are wrong" but "you have mistaken a theory of social *form* for a set of trend forecasts" — the same move the *neue Marx-Lektüre* makes against neoclassical readings of Marx (your MPE syllabus, opening note). Worth tracking as an early instance of the recurring category-collision between prediction and form-critique.

## MODULE 2: LENIN AND BOLSHEVISM — ORGANISATION, IMPERIALISM, THE STATE (1902–1923)

**Why this matters:** Lenin is the hinge of the entire tradition: after him, "Marxism" means, for most of the world for most of the century, *his* answers to the organisation question, the state question, and the imperialism question. You need him in the original because the codified "Leninism" of Module 4 is a distortion of him, and because two of his texts (the *State and Revolution* and the *Philosophical Notebooks*) are genuinely first-rate.

## MODULE 3: THE REVOLUTIONARY CONJUNCTURE AND THE BIRTH OF WESTERN MARXISM (1919–1923)

**Why this matters:** This is the **hinge module** into your Appendix C. The founding texts of Western Marxism (Lukács, Korsch) and the young Gramsci were written *inside* the revolutionary wave of 1919–23 as political interventions — and only became "philosophy" after that wave broke. Reading them here, in their conjuncture, is what makes your Appendix C legible as something other than academic reflection. The module's organising axis is **council vs. party** — the deepest fault line in revolutionary strategy, and the ancestor of the autonomy-vs-vanguard question in your operaismo material (Appendix C.VI).

Here read it as a *Comintern intervention*: the theory of "imputed" (*zugerechnetes*) class consciousness and of the party as the "form of mediation" between the class's empirical and its possible consciousness — the philosophical high-point of the vanguard idea. ## MODULE 4: TROTSKY, STALIN, AND THE FATE OF THE REVOLUTION (1924–1940)

**Why this matters:** Once power is held and the European revolution fails to arrive, Marxism splits over what the Soviet state *is* and what to do next: Trotsky's internationalist "permanent revolution" against Stalin's "socialism in one country," and — running underneath both — the codification of a living body of argument into *Marxismus-Leninismus* as state doctrine. This is where "did it work / what did it become?" becomes unavoidable, and where your Kołakowski (Appendix C.13) is the indispensable critical companion.

| # | Text | Type | Est. h | Notes |
|---|------|------|--------|-------|
| 4.1 | Leon Trotsky, *Ergebnisse und Perspektiven* (*Results and Prospects*, 1906) + *Die permanente Revolution* (*The Permanent Revolution*, 1929) | [P] | 8–12 | The theory of **permanent revolution** resting on **uneven and combined development** (*ungleiche und kombinierte Entwicklung*): a backward country can telescope historical stages, so the proletariat may take power *before* the bourgeois-democratic tasks are complete — but can only hold it if the revolution spreads internationally. The most theoretically fertile idea in the Bolshevik corpus. |
| 4.2 | Trotsky, *Die verratene Revolution* (*The Revolution Betrayed*, 1936) | [P] | 8–12 | The founding left analysis of "actually existing socialism": the USSR as a *degenerated workers' state* — nationalised property (a gain) captured by a privileged, unaccountable bureaucratic *stratum* (not a new ruling class) whose rule is unstable. A genuine sociological thesis, and the origin of one whole camp in the Debates section. |
| 4.3 | J. V. Stalin, *Über die Grundlagen des Leninismus* (*The Foundations of Leninism*, 1924) + *Über dialektischen und historischen Materialismus* (1938) | [P] **[SYM]** | 4–6 | The codification. *Foundations* freezes Lenin into "Leninism" (a fixed doctrine, "the Marxism of the epoch of imperialism"); the 1938 essay (from the *Short Course*) fixes *Diamat* (dialectical materialism) and *Histomat* as a numbered catechism — the four "features" of the dialectic, the five modes of production. **Read strictly as symptom**: this *is* what "Marxism-Leninism" became as the ideology of a state, and it is the thing Bloch, Sartre, Lefebvre, and the Frankfurt School were reacting against. | 4.4 | Stalin, *Marxismus und die nationale Frage* (1913) | [RS] | 3–4 | The definition of "nation" (common language, territory, economy, psychological make-up) that became Soviet nationalities policy — written under Lenin's direction, before the codification. The one early-Stalin text with genuine argumentative content, and relevant to the national-question thread (2.5 → Module 5). |
| 4.5 | Evgeny Preobrazhensky, *Die neue Ökonomik* (*The New Economics*, 1926), selections; with Bukharin as his opponent in the industrialisation debate | [RS] | 6–9 | The transition-*strategy* debate: Preobrazhensky's "primitive socialist accumulation" (*primitive sozialistische Akkumulation*) — the state extracting a surplus from the peasantry to fund industry — against Bukharin's gradualist, market-mediated worker-peasant alliance. Strategy, not equilibrium economics, so it lives here rather than in MPE. `[→ spine B]` |
| 4.6 | Trotsky, *Geschichte der russischen Revolution* (*History of the Russian Revolution*, 1930) | [O] | 20–30 | The literary-historical monument — and the fullest narrative embodiment of uneven-and-combined-development as a *method* of historical explanation. Orientation-depth; read selectively (the February and October volumes) or as reference. |

**[Economist's note]** The Preobrazhensky–Bukharin industrialisation debate (4.5) is one you will recognise as *your own subject in disguise*: it is a two-sector accumulation problem — how fast to squeeze agriculture to build capital goods — later formalised by Feldman (1928) and echoed in Domar-type and Mahalanobis growth planning. Preobrazhensky's "primitive socialist accumulation" is a savings-extraction mechanism with an explicit intertemporal trade-off and a political-feasibility constraint (the *smychka*, the worker-peasant alliance). Reading it as a growth-and-planning problem, and then noting what that framing *omits* (the coercion the model's "extraction" term conceals — which is where Kołakowski's critique bites), is a productive exercise squarely on your terrain. `[→ spine B]`

## MODULE 5: MAO AND THE MARXISM OF THE PERIPHERY (1927–1976)

**Why this matters:** Mao is the tradition's answer to a question Lenin only opened: what does revolution look like in an agrarian, semi-colonial society where the proletariat is tiny? The answer — peasant base areas, protracted people's war, the class *bloc* rather than the class — reorients Marxism toward the Global South and produces the century's other great state-socialist experiment. Two of the philosophical essays (*On Practice*, *On Contradiction*) are genuinely read for their dialectics; the rest is strategy and, later, the catastrophe of the Cultural Revolution.

| # | Text | Type | Est. h | Notes |

## MODULE 6: THE SOCIALIST "WOMAN QUESTION" AND REVOLUTIONARY FEMINISM

**Why this matters:** This is where Clara Zetkin properly lives, and it is a genuine bridge into two of your own strands. The classical socialist "*Frauenfrage*" (woman question) — Engels, Bebel, Zetkin, Kollontai — is the *ancestor* that your 1970s materialist feminists (Federici, Dalla Costa & James; Appendix C.16–C.17) both build on and attack, and it reaches directly into spine E (socialisation / *Bildung* / the family as a formative institution). Read it as the tradition's most serious attempt to think reproduction, the family, and sexuality as political-economic questions — and note precisely where it subordinates the woman question to the class question, since that subordination is exactly what the later feminists refuse.

## FRONTIER: LIVE DEBATES

Modelled on the "Frontier" sections of your companion syllabis: each is a question with its opposing camps and the texts (mostly already assigned) that fight it out. Work any one as a position-mapping exercise (Practice D, debate map).

**1. Spontaneity vs. organisation.** Luxemburg's mass strike (1.3) and *GuK*'s imputed consciousness (3.1) vs. Lenin's vanguard (2.1); the council communists (3.5) as the third position. Its living afterlife: the autonomy-vs-vanguard question in your operaismo material (Tronti, Appendix C.14) and the "leaderless" movements of the 2010s.

**2. Reform vs. revolution.** Bernstein/Kautsky/Luxemburg (Module 1) → Eurocommunism → **Erik Olin Wright's ruptural / interstitial / symbiotic strategies** (your Analytical-Marxism 4.3) → **Honneth, *Die Idee des Sozialismus* (2015)**. Map the classical trilemma onto Wright's typology and ask what, if anything, is new.

**3. Smash or capture the state?** Lenin's *State and Revolution* (2.3) vs. the **Miliband–Poulantzas debate** (1969–76) on the capitalist state's relative autonomy, and Eurocommunism's "democratic road." Connects to the *Staatsableitung* (state-derivation) literature flagged in your MPE Module 7 — where the derivation of the state-form stops short of the questions this debate forces.

**4. What was the USSR?** The tradition's deepest self-examination, and the one with a live economic dimension:
- **Degenerated workers' state** — Trotsky (4.2): nationalised property, bureaucratic *deformation*, not a new class.
- **State capitalism** — Tony Cliff, *State Capitalism in Russia* (1955/74); C. L. R. James & Raya Dunayevskaya (the Johnson-Forest Tendency): the bureaucracy *is* a capitalist class, the plan a form of capital.
- **Bureaucratic collectivism** — Bruno Rizzi, Max Shachtman: a new, non-capitalist, non-socialist mode of production.
- **The system as the logic of the doctrine** — Kołakowski (your C.13): Stalinism as a possible, perhaps probable, unfolding of Marxism itself, not a betrayal of it.
Van der Linden (0.5) is the map. *[Economist's note: the state-capitalism vs. degenerated-workers'-state dispute turns partly on whether "the law of value" operates internally in a planned economy — a question with a real analytic core (shadow prices, the socialist-calculation debate, the soft budget constraint à la Kornai) that an economist can adjudicate more precisely than the disputants did.]*

**5. Uneven and combined development.** Trotsky's concept (4.1) has had a major revival in historical sociology and International Relations — **Justin Rosenberg**'s programme; **Alex Anievas & Kerem Nişancıoğlu, *How the West Came to Rule* (2015)**. The one classical-Bolshevik idea currently generating frontier academic work; the natural extension if a contribution turned toward global/historical-sociological themes. (~4–6 h if pursued.)

**6. The national / colonial question and its afterlife.** Lenin (2.5) → Stalin's definition (4.4) → Mao/Fanon (Module 5) → dependency and world-systems theory, which hands off to **MPE Module 4**. The thread along which Marxism became a theory of the Global South.

## A NOTE ON RELATIONSHIP TO THE FRANKFURT TRADITION

Three tensions worth tracking, since this tradition is the one your main programme most silently presupposes:

**1. The lost object.** On Anderson's thesis (your C.0.1), the entire Western-Marxist tradition you are studying is what Marxism *became* once it was severed from a living revolutionary practice — it turned to philosophy and culture *because* the political road (this syllabus) had closed. Reading the revolutionary tradition is therefore not a detour from the Frankfurt School but an excavation of its condition of possibility and its bad conscience. Adorno's refusal of praxis, Habermas's turn to communication, Honneth's to recognition — each is, among other things, a stance toward the failure documented here.

**2. The bracketed questions.** The Frankfurt tradition is strong on diagnosis (what is pathological about this form of life?) and comparatively silent on agency and transition (who changes it, and how?). This tradition is the reverse. A Phase III contribution that stays purely diagnostic can ignore the gap; one that reaches for "and therefore…" — as Honneth's *Die Idee des Sozialismus* does — walks straight back into the terrain of Modules 1–4. Know where the questions were last seriously posed.

**3. The genre warning, restated.** Do not import the reading protocols of the main programme wholesale. A four-part Practice A reconstruction is the right tool for Hegel, Adorno, Habermas; it is the *wrong* tool for a strategic pamphlet whose claims are indexical to a conjuncture ("in *this* situation, *this* is the correct tactic"). For the [P] texts here, the more apt practices are Practice D (debate map: reconstruct the strategic *disagreement*, not one position's "argument") and, for the [SYM] texts, a Practice H1-style discrimination note (what is this a symptom *of*?). The tradition punishes readers who mistake it for philosophy — that is the first thing it can teach a philosopher.
